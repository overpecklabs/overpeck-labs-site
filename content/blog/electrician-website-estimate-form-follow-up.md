*Charlie and his company, Your Electric Co., are a fictional sample business we created for our videos. Jordan, the website visitors and the sample site are made up, not real clients or testimonials.*

Charlie is a Bergen County electrician, and it's 1:30 in the afternoon. He's on a job across town, and he isn't thinking about his website.

His website, though, is doing its job. This post follows the short video "Website," part of the series built around our commercial, [A Day in the Life of a Bergen County Electrician](/blog/a-day-in-the-life-of-a-bergen-county-electrician). It explains what happens between a homeowner finding your site and you picking up the job, in plain English.

## Step one: the estimate form

Jordan lives in Paramus and wants an EV charger installed in his garage. He lands on Charlie's website and sees a button: **Request an estimate**. The form asks for what you'd ask on a first phone call: his name, his town, his phone number and email, and a short description of the job.

Jordan types it in and taps **Send request**. The form confirms that it went through, so he isn't left wondering whether anything happened.

A good estimate form is short. Every extra field is one more reason for a homeowner to give up halfway, so the form asks for the details Charlie needs to call back with context and nothing else.

## Step two: the first response

This is the part the video is really about. As soon as Jordan sends the form, two things happen:

- **Jordan gets a text and an email.** The text reads: "Thanks, Jordan! We got your request for an EV charger install in Paramus. We'll reach out to schedule your estimate." He knows his request landed, and he knows what happens next.
- **Charlie's phone gets a "New estimate request" alert.** He sees it when he's between jobs, with the name, town and job already filled in.

Charlie isn't typing replies from a job site. The first reply goes out on its own, and the real conversation (pricing, scheduling, site details) still belongs to Charlie.

That first reply matters because a person who has just sent a request is waiting. A quick, friendly acknowledgment tells them the business is real and they haven't been ignored.

## Step three: the website chat

Some visitors don't want to fill out a form yet. They have a question first. In the video, a visitor asks the site chat: "Do you install EV chargers in Ridgewood?"

The chat answers: "Yes, we install home EV chargers across Bergen County. The best next step is a free estimate request." Then the **Request an estimate** button lights up.

That's a deliberately narrow job:

- **It answers common questions** about services and the areas you cover, based on information you provide.
- **It points visitors to the estimate form.**
- **It doesn't book jobs, quote prices or diagnose problems.** Those stay with you.

Think of it as a polite front-desk sign that can talk. It saves a visitor from hunting through your site, and it moves them toward the one action that matters.

## What this adds up to

Put the pieces together and Charlie's website does a few specific things while he works:

1. It collects a clean estimate request with the details he needs.
2. It sends the customer a text and an email so they know it arrived.
3. It alerts Charlie so he can follow up himself.
4. It answers simple questions and points people to the form.

None of that replaces a phone call or a site visit. It makes the first few minutes after a homeowner reaches out feel responsive, and it hands Charlie a well-organized request instead of a half-remembered voicemail.

## What to check on your own site

If you're a trade owner, here's a quick audit of your current website:

- **Is there a clear estimate button** above the fold on a phone?
- **Does the form confirm** that the request was sent?
- **Does the customer get a text or email** afterward?
- **Do you get an alert** that includes the job details?
- **Does the site load fast on a phone?** Test it on your own phone.

Anywhere you can't answer yes is a good place to start.
